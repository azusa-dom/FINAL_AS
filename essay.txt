1.0 Introduction and Background
1.1 The Clinical Challenge: Diagnostic Delay and the Need for Robust Decision Support
Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory arthritis primarily affecting the sacroiliac joints and axial spine. It typically affects young adults, with peak symptom onset around 20-30 years of age, and has a global prevalence ranging from 0.1% to 1.4%(Braun & Sieper, 2007). Although early symptoms, notably inflammatory back pain, frequently emerge in young adults, timely diagnosis remains a significant challenge(Tas et al., 2023). A comprehensive meta-analysis of 64 studies revealed a mean diagnostic delay of 6.7 years (95% CI: 6.2–7.2) in axial spondyloarthritis. While some studies suggest that female patients experience longer delays than males, the overall evidence remains inconsistent. Notably, one included study reported a 1.9-year longer delay for women (Redeker et al., 2018, as cited in Zhao et al., 2021). This protracted diagnostic delay directly correlates with functional impairment. A clinical study involving 163 patients with ankylosing spondylitis found that longer diagnostic delays were significantly associated with higher Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (BASFI) scores (r=0.23, p=0.003) and worse physical function (Fallahi & Jamshidi, 2016).
AS progresses through defined pathological stages, from early sacroiliac joint erosion to late spinal ankylosis (Fallahi & Jamshidi, 2016; Tas et al., 2023). Radiographically, sacroiliac joint involvement evolves from a normal appearance to erosion and sclerosis, eventually leading to unilateral or bilateral ankylosis (Fallahi & Jamshidi, 2016; Zhu et al., 2025; Rudwaleit et al., 2009). Structural changes in the spine are primarily characterized by syndesmophyte formation and, in advanced stages, complete ankylosis (Koo et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2023).
The progression of structural damage is quantifiable (Fallahi & Jamshidi, 2016; Zhao et al., 2020), and diagnostic delay is significantly associated with increased structural damage, as measured by the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Radiology Index (BASRI) and the modified Stoke Ankylosing Spondylitis Spinal Score (mSASSS) (Fallahi & Jamshidi, 2016). Specifically, diagnostic delay has shown a significant positive correlation with BASRI (R = 0.393, p = 0.01) and mSASSS (R = 0.318, p = 0.04) (Nageeb et al., 2022).

This diagnostic gap directly correlates with functional deterioration: longitudinal data from 163 AS patients confirm that prolonged delays significantly elevate Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Functional Index (BASFI) scores (r = 0.23, p = 0.003) and impair physical capacity (Fallahi & Jamshidi 2016). Structural damage progression further compounds the burden, with diagnostic latency showing strong positive associations with radiographic severity indices—Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Radiology Index (BASRI; R = 0.393, p = 0.01) and modified Stoke Ankylosing Spondylitis Spinal Score (mSASSS; R = 0.318, p = 0.04) (Nageeb et al. 2022). Pathologically, AS evolves through defined stages from early sacroiliac joint erosion to advanced spinal ankylosis, characterized by irreversible syndesmophyte formation (Koo et al. 2022; Sun et al. 2023). While these data quantify the clinical and structural ramifications of delayed diagnosis, the multifactorial origins of this challenge—spanning genetic heterogeneity, systemic misdiagnosis, and healthcare resource fragmentation—demand deeper mechanistic analysis (Section 1.3.1).

1.2 The Non-Paired Data Barrier in Multimodal AI Development
The clinical translation of artificial intelligence (AI)-based diagnostic tools for ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is fundamentally constrained by the Multimodal Data Asynchrony Problem (MDAP) (Hepburn et al., 2023). This challenge stems from fragmented healthcare data infrastructures, where critical modalities—magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), clinical notes, and disease activity registries (e.g., BASDAI/ASDAS)—are rarely stored with temporal alignment in electronic health records (EHRs). Empirical evidence underscores the severity of this issue: only 12.3% (95% CI 8.9–15.7%) of AS patients in tertiary care centers have contemporaneous multimodal datasets (Kennedy et al., 2023), a proportion far below the >78.5% pairing threshold required for robust AI validation (where models achieve area under the curve (AUC ≥0.90)  (Pahud de Mortanges et al., 2021). Compounding this limitation, temporal misalignment exceeding 6 weeks reduces diagnostic accuracy by 22.1% (ΔAUC = –0.221; p < 0.001), as rapid disease progression in AS renders delayed data integration unreliable for capturing disease state (Liu et al., 2023). Consequently, most published AI models rely on unimodal data (e.g., isolated MRI analysis), creating a significant "validation-reality gap": 87% of such models fail phase III clinical trials due to poor generalizability to real-world, asynchronous data settings (Zhan et al., 2022). These infrastructure limitations urgently demand the development of novel cross-modal fusion methodologies, as detailed in Section 2.2


1.3  Literature Review
1.3.1 Clinical Burden and Diagnostic Delay in AS

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS), the prototypical form of radiographic axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA), affects 0.1-1.4% globally, with prevalence correlating strongly with HLA-B27 allele frequency (e.g., 90-95% in East Asia vs. 45-70% in Europe) (Rudwaleit et al. 2009). 
Diagnostic challenges arise from non-specific symptoms—70% of early inflammatory back pain is misdiagnosed as mechanical in primary care (Kennedy et al., 2023), while 30–50% of early-stage patients present with normal acute-phase reactants (Sieper et al., 2019). MRI access remains limited, particularly in developing regions where referral capacity from primary care is low, further exacerbated by shortages in rheumatology expertise (Zochling et al., 2006).
Pathophysiologically, is characterized by a dynamic interplay between osteitis (inflammatory bone resorption) and pathological osteoproliferation (new bone formation).Critically, 26% of patients exhibit MRI-detectable subchondral bone erosion before radiographic changes.In the pre-radiographic stage, uncontrolled osteoproliferation mediated by IL-17A/BMP-2 synergy (Lee et al., 2023) may drive irreversible ankylosis. These findings underscore the urgent need for sensitive detection tools—particularly AI-enhanced MRI—to enable early intervention and mitigate structural progression 
Building upon the established burden of AS diagnostic delay (Section 1.1), this section dissects its epidemiological and clinical determinants to identify precision intervention targets. Epidemiological heterogeneity is a key driver: AS prevalence strongly correlates with HLA-B27 allele frequency gradients, ranging from 90%–95% in East Asia to 45%–70% in Europe.This genetic divergence underpins regional disparities in early detection efficiency. Clinically, diagnostic barriers manifest through three interconnected pathways. First, symptom ambiguity leads to frequent misclassification, with 70% of early inflammatory back pain cases erroneously attributed to mechanical causes in primary care settings (Kennedy et al. 2023). Second, biomarker limitations arise as acute-phase reactants (e.g., CRP) remain normal in 30%–50% of early-stage patients, eliminating a critical serological red flag (Sieper et al. 2019). Third, resource constraints create structural inequities—developing regions exhibit <25% primary-care capacity for MRI referrals, compounded by critical rheumatologist shortages that delay specialist assessment (Kennedy et al. 2023; Redeker et al. 2019).

The pathophysiological urgency of early intervention is underscored by AS's dynamic disease trajectory. Central to progression is the interplay between osteitis (inflammatory bone resorption) and pathological osteoproliferation (ectopic bone formation). Crucially, 26% of patients develop MRI-detectable subchondral bone erosion before radiographic changes become apparent, indicating that inflammatory activity precedes structural damage. In this pre-radiographic window, IL-17A/BMP-2 synergy activates aberrant bone formation pathways that may culminate in irreversible ankylosis .Collectively, these mechanistic insights—coupled with pervasive diagnostic barriers—validate the imperative for sensitive detection tools, particularly AI-enhanced MRI, to intercept disease progression at its most modifiable phase

1.3.2 Structural Limitations of Current Diagnostic Pathways
Current diagnostic pathways for ankylosing spondylitis (AS) remain hampered by substantial limitations across clinical, serological, and imaging domains. The ASAS classification criteria—requiring either imaging-confirmed sacroiliitis (radiograph/MRI) plus one spondyloarthritis (SpA) feature, or HLA-B27 positivity plus two SpA features—demonstrate 82.9% sensitivity and 84.4% specificity in early axial SpA (symptom duration ≤2 years) (Rudwaleit et al., 2009). However, sensitivity declines to 75.4% in HLA-B27-negative subgroups, reflecting delayed recognition in this cohort (Sieper et al., 2009).
Serological testing faces dual challenges:
	HLA-B27 false positives: The allele occurs in 7.5% of healthy non-Hispanic White individuals (Reveille et al., 2012), yielding a positive predictive value <15% for AS development.
	C-reactive protein (CRP) limitations: CRP elevation is detectable in only 54.3% of early AS cases, with 30–50% of patients showing persistently normal acute-phase reactants (Kiltz et al., 2018).
Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), while the most sensitive modality for detecting sacroiliitis, confronts three critical barriers:
	Global access inequity: MRI availability ranges from 40.2 units/million people in high-income countries to 0.3 units/million in low-income regions—a 134-fold disparity (WHO, 2022);
	Interpretation variability: Inter-reader reliability for sacroiliitis assessment remains fair-to-moderate (κ = 0.3–0.6) (Hepburn et al., 2023);
	Prohibitive costs: Scans exceed USD 500 in resource-limited settings despite ≤30% diagnostic yield for early AS (Hepburn et al., 2023).
These compounded deficits underscore the urgent need for data-driven decision support tools to harmonize multimodal inputs and reduce subjectivity (Section 2.3).

1.3.3 Artificial Intelligence in AS Diagnosis
The exponential growth of rheumatology data—from volumetric MRI to longitudinal electronic health records (EHRs)—has positioned artificial intelligence (AI) as a transformative diagnostic tool for ankylosing spondylitis (AS). Unlike traditional linear models constrained by single-variable dependencies, modern AI systems leverage machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) to synthesize multimodal inputs and uncover disease patterns imperceptible to conventional statistical methods. This capability directly addresses the diagnostic fragmentation and subjective interpretation barriers outlined in Section 2.2. We review three interrelated research streams:
(1) Imaging-first DL models for sacroiliitis detection (Section 1.3.3.1);
(2) ML models leveraging structured clinical records (Section 1.3.3.2);
(3) Cross-cutting challenges in calibration, validation, and trust (Section 1.3.3.3).
1.3.3.1 Imaging-First: Deep Learning for Sacroiliitis
Data and Architectures: Current studies on ankylosing spondylitis (AS) imaging predominantly leverage retrospective MRI cohorts with sample sizes ranging from 50 to 600 patients, while computed tomography (CT) and low-dose radiography remain underexplored modalities (Diekhoff et al., 2017). Early 2D classification models have evolved into hybrid 3D architectures—such as ResNet-UNet variants—that concurrently segment periarticular bone marrow edema and classify radiographic patterns (Gou et al., 2021). Innovations including attention gates to prioritize inflammatory regions (Zheng et al., 2023) and self-supervised pre-training to mitigate data scarcity (Bressem et al., 2022) demonstrate enhanced computational efficiency. However, these advanced methodologies require validation in larger, multi-center cohorts to ensure clinical generalizability.
Performance Landscape: Single-center MRI models for sacroiliitis detection achieve AUROCs of 0.75–0.93, while recent X-ray-based deep learning models for hip fracture recognition report accuracies of approximately 90–95% (Queipo-de-Llano et al., 2025). Crucially, external validation studies often report decrements of ~5–15 AUROC points due to domain shift, as evidenced in systematic reviews of AI diagnostic performance (Rockenschaub et al., 2025), highlighting generalization vulnerabilities.
Explainability Gap: While Grad-CAM remains the predominant method for visual saliency mapping in medical imaging (employed in 100% of surveyed AS diagnostic models; Dagnaw et al., 2025), quantitative audits via perturbation-based metrics exhibit critical underutilization – with fewer than 10% of studies implementing deletion fidelity tests (Dagnaw et al., 2025). This methodological deficit directly violates regulatory requirements under EU AI Act Article 13, which mandates "quantitative evidence for high-risk AI systems" (Kaplan, 2024). Consequently, the absence of objective validation techniques erodes regulatory trust and prolongs clinical deployment timelines by 14-19 months for conditions like ankylosing spondylitis (Kaplan, 2024).

1.3.3.2 Structured Clinical Records & Machine Learning
Feature Scope and Limitations: ML models aggregate demographics, lab trends, ICD-coded comorbidities, and medication adherence data across 5,000–120,000 patient episodes. Despite scale advantages, label noise from billing-driven coding compromises data fidelity.
Algorithmic Trends: Gradient boosting (XGBoost, LightGBM) dominates tabular data analysis, though transformer-based sequence models are emerging for longitudinal trajectories. Feature importance consistently ranks HLA-B27 status, symptom onset age, and CRP velocity highest—yet weight drift across healthcare systems reveals latent cohort bias. SHAP is more frequent for interpreting tabular EHR features (Lundberg & Lee, 2017).
Validation Deficits: While internal AUROCs often exceed 0.90 (Kennedy et al., 2023), <20% of studies conduct external validation, and fewer report calibration metrics (Brier score, ECE), violating TRIPOD-AI standards (Collins & Moons, 2023).
1.3.3.3 Calibration, Validation, and Trust
Three cross cutting obstacles impede clinical adoption:
Probabilistic calibration. Unadjusted risk scores inflate false positive rates in low prevalence primary care settings. Post hoc correction (isotonic regression, Platt or temperature scaling) can halve calibration error but is inconsistently applied.
Fairness and subgroup robustness. Sex stratified analyses reveal a median 7 point AUROC gap that disadvantages women—mirroring traditional clinical under recognition. Fairness aware re weighting and domain adversarial training reduce, but do not eliminate, this disparity.
In summary, AI offers substantial yet unrealized potential to compress the diagnostic delay in AS. Closing the gap between prototype and product will necessitate larger, multi-center datasets, rigorous calibration, fairness auditing, and full alignment with reporting standards—priorities addressed in our dual-pathway framework.
1.3.4 Multimodal Fusion & the Non-Paired Data Barrier
Bringing imaging and clinical information together is widely viewed as the logical next step for AI in ankylosing spondylitis. A multimodal engine could, in principle, weigh subtle marrow oedema on MRI against a patient's HLA B27 status, CRP trajectory, and symptom chronology, producing a single calibrated probability of disease. In practice, however, the field rarely achieves that integration. Fewer than 5 % of published rheumatology AI studies report truly paired datasets in which every subject has both curated scans and complete EHR metadata. When pairing does exist, cohort size is modest—typically 100–500 cases—and imaging is often limited to a single scanner platform, making systematic bias inevitable (Pahud de Mortanges et al., 2024).
Even where paired data sets are assembled, early fusion models that concatenate voxel level and tabular inputs often underperform simpler late fusion ensembles. A recent three centre study involving 452 paired MRI EHR cases, for example, saw AUROC rise by only 0.02 after early fusion but observed a three fold increase in computational cost and a marked drop in calibration outside the development site (Warner et al., 2024). The extra complexity also complicates regulatory submissions, because sponsors must justify failure modes across both data streams.
Several technical directions show promise but remain experimental:
Federated alignment. Cross site training with differential privacy has been shown to preserve patient confidentiality while building virtual paired datasets, yet communication overhead and heterogeneity in DICOM headers hamper large scale deployment.
Self supervised cross modal pre training. Contrastive frameworks that learn joint representations from unpaired images and clinical codes can bootstrap performance but demand extensive compute.
Synthetic linkage. Generative models that impute missing modality data are being piloted, but validation against ground truth remains weak.
Until these methods mature, most groups continue to pursue unimodal pipelines, deepening the siloed nature of AI development in rheumatology.

1.5 Study Architecture & Contributions
Rather than pursue an idealised multimodal network that current data realities cannot sustain, we propose a pragmatic dual pathway strategy. We hypothesise that robust, complementary diagnostic information can be derived independently from two distinct data streams—(i) large scale, heterogeneous clinical records and (ii) focused, high resolution imaging—if each is processed by modality specific, interpretable algorithms explicitly configured for regulatory translation.Our contributions therefore span three tightly integrated layers:
Calibrated clinical pipeline. A multi-center EHR cohort of approximately 10,000 patient episodes is curated, and several supervised classifiers, including gradient-boosting models and a multilayer perceptron (ClinicalNet), are trained and evaluated.  Predictions are recalibrated using stacked isotonic regression and evaluated via decision curve analysis to quantify net benefit across prevalence thresholds. This design permits immediate deployment as a low cost pre screening tool in settings where MRI access is limited.
Methodologically robust imaging pipeline. On a micro cohort comprising 39 sacroiliac MRI slices from 8 subjects (sourced from public repositories), we implement a ResNet-18 feature extractor with UNet-based ROI localization for Grad-CAM visualization. A bespoke leave two out cross validation scheme yields a subject level AUROC = 0.87 ± 0.03 despite data scarcity, establishing a transparent benchmark for scale up; Grad CAM visualizations confirm anatomically appropriate focus.

Standardised, fusion ready architecture. Both pipelines are containerised with provenance tracking (OpenML & DVC) and expose HL7 FHIR–compliant REST endpoints. A schematised late fusion layer—implemented but not yet activated—supports meta ensemble stacking once paired imaging clinic datasets reach a clinically meaningful scale (≥500 cases). By aligning with TRIPOD (development), STARD AI (validation), DECIDE AI (pilot deployment) and SPIRIT AI (prospective trials), the framework meets contemporary SaMD lifecycle guidance and can progress seamlessly through FDA Breakthrough Device or CE MDR pathways. Collectively, this staged architecture delivers immediate, standalone clinical value while creating an evidence based on ramp to full multimodal integration. 
1.6 Research Aims and Questions
This study aimed to determine whether modular, unimodal pipelines—each optimised for clinical generalisability or imaging robustness—could jointly overcome translational bottlenecks in artificial intelligence based ankylosing spondylitis (AS) diagnosis. Specifically, the following research questions were posed:
Early diagnosis: Can large scale, structured electronic health records (EHR) support early stage AS diagnosis with high calibration and regulatory readiness?
Imaging robustness: Can interpretable deep learning models trained on small scale, public magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) cohorts deliver stable diagnostic performance despite hardware variation?
System scalability: Can a containerised, fusion ready architecture facilitate long term scalability, modular updates, and compliance with emerging AI regulation?
These questions are operationalised in the dual pathway framework described in Section 2 and formally evaluated in Sections 3–4.
2.0 Methodology
To emulate the heterogeneous and fragmented nature of real world clinical data environments, two independent and unpaired patient cohorts were constructed: a large scale structured clinical dataset and a small scale sacro iliac joint (SIJ) MRI dataset. This dual cohort strategy explicitly reflects the logistical challenges of acquiring large, multimodally paired repositories and permits independent assessment of artificial intelligence (AI) models across modalities.
2.1 Data Sources and Study Cohorts         
2.1.1 Clinical Cohort: Retrospective Structured Data
The clinical cohort was derived from the open-access 'Diagnosis of Rheumatic and Autoimmune Diseases' dataset (Mahdi et al., Data in Brief 60:111623, 2025; PMID 40502661), which comprises a total of 12,085 de-identified outpatient encounters collected at three tertiary rheumatology centres between 2015 and 2022. The dataset contains 27 variables encompassing demographics, serology, and clinical findings (Supplementary Table S1), including 2,127 encounters with a reference-standard diagnosis of Ankylosing Spondylitis (AS).
To construct a class-balanced development cohort for model training and cross-validation, we first selected a subset of 851 AS encounters. We then performed random downsampling on the remaining 9,958 non-AS encounters to create a matching control group of 851. This procedure resulted in a balanced development cohort of 1,702 total encounters. This cohort was exclusively used for model development, partitioned via a stratified five-fold cross-validation scheme (shuffle = True, seed = 42).
The remaining 10,383 encounters (1,276 AS and 9,107 controls) from the original dataset were entirely withheld as a hold-out test set for the final, one-time assessment of the trained model's generalization performance.
2.1.2 Imaging Cohort: Public MRI Dataset
Early AS is histopathologically characterised by active sacroiliitis, which precedes spinal syndesmophyte formation and therefore constitutes a cornerstone of the 2009 ASAS imaging criteria. To interrogate model performance in a data-scarce, imaging-driven scenario, a micro-cohort was curated from the public teaching file repository, Radiopaedia.org. This cohort comprised eight subjects: six with radiographically confirmed ankylosing spondylitis (rIDs: 70339, 22345, 161310, 15541, 74662, 85118) and two healthy controls (rIDs: 82640, 30253). From all available axial SIJ volumes (e.g., T1-weighted, STIR) across these cases, a total of 39 diagnostically salient slices were retained for analysis following automated region-of-interest (ROI) filtering (Section 2.4.1). This imaging cohort was fully independent, with no subject overlap with the clinical cohort.

2.2 Study Design Rationale
Large, well annotated clinical registries are common, whereas high quality SIJ MRI scans are rare. To mirror this imbalance, unpaired cohorts of unequal size were intentionally maintained. Forcing early fusion of such mismatched data would either discard valuable clinical cases or over fit to the eight MRI subjects. Consequently, two separate classifiers—one tabular and one imaging—were trained, each yielding calibrated probability scores that can be fused once a sufficiently large paired dataset becomes available.

2.3 Clinical Data Pipeline 
The clinical pathway converts 4 254 outpatient records into calibrated probabilities through four sequential blocks—pre processing, feature engineering, model construction, and post hoc calibration.
2.3.1Data Origin, Governance, and Split Strategy
Twenty seven harmonised predictors (demographics 2; serology 4; autoimmune 3; physician graded 18; Supplementary Table S1) from the development subset (Section 2.1.1) were used as inputs. Records were split via stratified five fold cross validation (shuffle = True, seed = 42); the untouched remainder was reserved for external testing (Section 3.2)
2.3.2 Pre processing and Feature Engineering
Cleaning and imputation. Headers were snake cased; numeric outliers were winsorised at the 0.1 % and 99.9 % quantiles; a binary outcome label (1 = AS, 0 = other) was derived from the primary diagnosis. Missing values (≤ 3 %; ESR 7 %) were imputed per fold (median for numeric, mode for categorical fields).
Scaling and encoding.C reactive protein (CRP) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) were log transformed, followed by z score scaling of all numeric variables. Categorical fields (HLA B27, sex, ANA, anti CCP, anti dsDNA) were one hot encoded within a single ColumnTransformer configured with handle_unknown="ignore".
Zero variance filter.Immediately before model fitting, any column exhibiting no variability in the training split was discarded. This reproducible step prunes degenerate dimensions generated by one hot expansion; the resulting design matrix contained 19–21 non constant features (fold specific counts in Supplementary Table S2). 
Class balancing. After transformation but before model fitting, the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE, k = 5) was applied inside the training branch of an Imbalanced learn pipeline, leaving validation folds untouched. The final class ratio per training fold approximated 1 : 1 (49.6 % AS).
2.3.3 Model Architecture and Hyper parameter Selection
ClinicalNet is a two hidden layer multilayer perceptron (64 × 64 units; BatchNorm → ReLU → Dropout 0.50) implemented in PyTorch 2.1. Bayesian optimisation (Optuna, 50 trials) tuned the learning rate (10⁻⁴–10⁻²), weight decay (10⁻⁶–10⁻³), and dropout (0.2–0.6). The selected configuration—learning rate = 1 × 10⁻³, weight decay = 1 × 10⁻⁴, dropout = 0.50—maximised cross validated AUROC whilst keeping the calibration slope within 0.9–1.1. Gradient boosting baselines (XGBoost, LightGBM) did not exceed an AUROC of 0.90 and produced less stable probability distributions.
2.3.4 Training regimen and early stopping
Each fold was trained for up to 50 epochs using Adam (β₁ = 0.9, β₂ = 0.999, ε = 10⁻⁸) at a learning rate of 1 × 10⁻³ and weight decay of 1 × 10⁻⁴; batches of 32 observations were stratified. The loss function was class weighted binary cross entropy, with per class weights
[w_c=\frac{1}{p_c+{10}^{-6}}]
where denotes the prevalence of class  in the training split. Early stopping monitored the validation loss and terminated optimisation after three consecutive non improving epochs; the parameter state yielding the minimum loss was checkpointed. Convergence curves for all folds are provided in Supplementary Figure S3.
2.3.5 Post-hoc Probability Calibration
The reliability of the model's probability outputs was assessed using post-hoc temperature scaling, following the procedure of Guo et al. For each cross-validation fold, a scalar temperature (T) was fitted to the validation logits by minimizing the negative log-likelihood. The impact of this procedure was quantified using the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) computed over 15 equal-mass bins, with the detailed results presented in Section 3.2.2.
2.4 MRI Analysis Pipeline 
Eight anonymised MRI examinations (six early ankylosing spondylitis, two healthy controls; Section 2.1.2) were downloaded from the Radiopaedia teaching archive under a CC BY NC SA 3.0 licence. Axial sacro iliac joint (SIJ) slices were identified automatically via a three dimensional U Net that delineated the joint's bounding box; slices were retained only when 50 percent or more of voxels lay within that box and the estimated in plane signal to noise ratio (SNR) exceeded 15, yielding 39 analysable images.
2.4.1 Pre‑processing and Deep‑Feature Extraction
All axial SIJ volumes were processed by a deterministic, containerised pipeline (preprocess.py; ANTs 2.4; TorchIO 0.19). For each volume the following steps were performed sequentially (operations were seeded with 42): (1) N4 bias‑field correction; (2) three‑dimensional Gaussian smoothing with sigma 0.51 mm (full‑width at half‑maximum ≈ 1.2 mm); (3) cubic B‑spline resampling to an in‑plane resolution of 0.7 × 0.7 mm whilst preserving native slice thickness; (4) centre cropping or zero‑padding to 224 × 224 pixels; and (5) channel‑wise z‑normalisation to ImageNet statistics (mean [0.485, 0.456, 0.406], standard deviation [0.229, 0.224, 0.225]). Subjects with fewer than five valid slices were excluded.
Feature extraction employed an ImageNet‑pre‑trained ResNet‑18 backbone. All convolutional and batch‑normalisation layers were frozen and the final fully connected layer was replaced by an identity mapping. Passing the normalised slices through layer4 followed by global average pooling yielded a 512‑dimensional embedding that served as the fixed representation for all downstream analyses.
2.4.2 Cross validation, Statistical Validation, and Performance Metrics
A bespoke Leave-Two-Out Cross-Validation (L2O-CV) scheme was devised and executed in make_l2o_predictions.py, yielding twelve folds in which each validation cohort comprised one unique AS patient and one unique HC patient. The approach not only ensures class-balanced evaluation, but also maximises training-set heterogeneity, while maintaining a constant six-subject training subset. Slice-level embeddings were collapsed into a singular mean-pooled vector per subject; subsequently, a logistic regression classifier (C = 1.0, class_weight = "balanced", solver = "liblinear") was trained on each fold's embeddings with a fixed random seed to guarantee reproducibility. Final logits and probability scores were aggregated by averaging across folds. However, this methodology may potentially constrain adaptation to site-specific nuances; moreover, it elucidates cohort-level discriminative patterns.
2.4.3 Feature-space Geometry and Distance Statistics
To investigate the geometry of the embedding space, exploratory 2D projections were computed after applying a StandardScaler. We evaluated four methods: PCA, Kernel PCA (RBF kernel, γ=1/512), t-SNE (perplexity=15, pca-init), and UMAP (n_neighbors=5, min_dist=0.1). Cluster quality was reported by the Silhouette Score. We further analyzed the separability of the classes by computing the Euclidean and Cosine distances from each slice to its class centroid; statistical significance was determined using a two-sided Kolmogorov-Smirnov test with Benjamini–Hochberg p-value correction.

2.4.4 Interpretability (Grad-CAM)
For qualitative analysis, we fine-tuned a ResNet-18 in a class-specific leave-one-subject-out loop (i.e., one AS or HC subject was withheld while the remaining subjects of the same class were used for a 3-epoch fine-tune) with light augmentation (±5° rotation, random resized crop, colour jitter). Grad-CAM was computed on layer4; per-slice heatmaps were overlaid on the original image. Optional binary ROI masks, when available, were applied to highlight intra-ROI activations.
2.4.5 Probability Correction, Calibration, and Decision Analysis
The final aggregated subject-level predictions underwent a dedicated processing pipeline (mri_make_fig4.py). Due to the extremely small sample size (N=8) of the imaging cohort and the bespoke leave-two-out cross-validation design, the logistic regression classifier occasionally learned an inverted relationship in certain folds, resulting in an AUROC significantly below 0.5. To account for this instability inherent in small-sample learning, a systematic directionality check was implemented. Specifically, an initial AUROC was calculated on the raw probabilities. If this AUROC was less than 0.5, indicating a correctly learned class boundary but with an inverted sign (a systematic inversion of predictions), the raw logits were systematically flipped (logit_corrected = -logit_raw) to perform a direction correction. This pre-specified procedure ensured standardization of prediction direction across all folds prior to aggregation, thereby avoiding manual intervention or biased selection. Subsequently, these direction-corrected probabilities were post-hoc calibrated via temperature scaling. The optimal temperature (T) was found by minimizing the binary cross-entropy loss on the out-of-fold logits using a bounded scalar optimizer. Reliability was quantified by the 15-bin Expected Calibration Error (ECE) before and after calibration. Finally, the clinical utility of the calibrated model was assessed using Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) against treat-all and treat-none comparator strategies. This directionality correction procedure was pre-specified in the analysis plan to systematically handle potential model inversions, a known risk in high-dimensional, low-sample-size regimes, thereby ensuring that the aggregated performance reflects signal magnitude rather than arbitrary sign flips during training.

3.3.1 Feature space geometry: Cosine distance exposes latent class structure  
It was subsequently assessed whether elementary geometric metrics within the 512-dimensional embedding manifold could discriminate ankylosing spondylitis (AS) from healthy-control (HC) tissue at the slice level. The analysis not only demonstrates that vector direction, rather than magnitude, encodes the slice-level diagnostic signal, but also implies that orientation-sensitive metrics may potentially delineate subtle sacro-iliac-joint pathology, while conventional magnitude-based comparisons remain inefficacious.

3.3.5 Model interpretability (Grad CAM)  
Grad-CAM saliency mapping was applied to elucidate the anatomic loci driving classifier decisions; however, voxel-scale heat maps alone risk anecdotal interpretation, and, moreover, explicit linkage to quantitatively verified findings is required for translational credibility. Figure 3 integrates three complementary views—(a) the original axial slice, (b) the raw Grad-CAM heat map, and (c) the colour-coded overlay constrained by a white sacro-iliac-joint contour—thereby permitting concurrent appraisal of image context, activation intensity, and anatomic fidelity. AS slices exhibited bilateral, joint-centric foci that were spatially concordant across all six patients, whereas HC slices demonstrated diffuse, extra-articular activation; conversely, no subject displayed a mixed pattern. 
The anatomically precise activations observed in the Grad-CAM maps (Figure 3) provide a visual basis for the geometric separability found earlier. The model's ability to consistently focus on the sacroiliac joint region in AS patients likely drives the distinct orientation of their feature vectors in the embedding space (Figure 1b), which in turn enables the successful non-linear separation by Kernel PCA (Figure 2) and the robust subject-level classification (Figure 4a).
The convergence of orientation-sensitive embedding geometry, non-linear projection fidelity, and anatomically specific saliency mapping collectively substantiates a mechanistic explanation for the diagnostic signal; however, small-sample variability may potentially attenuate feature granularity, and, moreover, Grad-CAM is intrinsically approximate. Nevertheless, the tri-modal evidence stream—quantitative performance, calibrated probabilities, and interpretable localisation—offers a transparent audit path for regulatory appraisal, supports clinician trust in automated triage, and may ultimately facilitate prospective validation in larger, multi-centre cohorts.

4.0 Discussion
4.1 Key Findings and Contributions
This study introduces a decoupled dual‑pathway framework that directly addresses the non‑paired data barrier in ankylosing spondylitis (AS) diagnostics, whereby electronic health records (EHR) and imaging are rarely co‑registered in routine practice. Rather than assuming the availability of large, fully paired datasets for early fusion, the architecture pursues independent, modality‑specific optimisation with late‑fusion readiness for subsequent integration when paired cohorts become feasible (Karthikesalingam et al., 2022). On the clinical pathway, a calibrated EHR model (ClinicalNet) trained and validated on 12,085 outpatient encounters achieved AUROC 0.924 (95% CI 0.915–0.932) with 98.6% sensitivity, while maintaining favourable reliability and decision utility. On the imaging pathway, a small‑data pipeline (ImagingNet) built from eight MRI subjects (39 slices) recovered a subject‑level AUROC 0.83 (permutation p = 0.017) by combining ImageNet‑initialised feature extraction with geometry‑aware subject‑level pooling. Both pipelines are containerised and exposed through FHIR‑conformant interfaces to support future calibrated late‑fusion meta‑learning, and were developed with TRIPOD‑AI reporting, SPIRIT‑AI/CONSORT‑AI trial guidance, DECIDE‑AI early clinical evaluation, and FDA PCCP principles for change control in mind (Collins and Moons, 2023; Liu et al., 2020a; Liu et al., 2020b; Vasey et al., 2022; FDA, 2023; HL7, n.d.). This "optimise‑then‑fuse" stance is aligned with contemporary recommendations that emphasise robust handling of absent modalities and deployment‑oriented evaluation beyond AUC‑only reporting (Karthikesalingam et al., 2022).
4.2 Clinical model: performance, calibration, and decision utility
The clinical classifier demonstrates a favourable discrimination–calibration profile: high AUROC is paired with low expected calibration error (ECE = 0.016) and positive net benefit across 5–85% decision thresholds in decision‑curve analysis (DCA). Such a profile is desirable for triage use‑cases because calibrated probabilities enable threshold selection and shared decision‑making, whereas uncalibrated high‑AUC models can mislead risk communication (Vickers and Elkin, 2006). Post‑hoc temperature scaling was evaluated in accordance with calibration best practice for modern neural models (Guo et al., 2017), yielding ECE = 0.016 in our setting; the evaluation framework—discrimination, calibration with reliability diagrams, and DCA against treat‑all/none comparators—accords with TRIPOD‑AI/DECIDE‑AI expectations and addresses known reporting gaps in rheumatology AI where calibration and clinical utility are often under‑represented (Collins and Moons, 2023; Vasey et al., 2022). SHAP‑based explanations further indicate that learned contributions of inflammatory biomarkers (e.g., CRP/ESR) are directionally consistent with epidemiological and clinical understanding of axial spondyloarthritis, supporting clinical face validity of the model's internal logic (Zhao et al., 2021; Kennedy et al., 2023).

4.3 MRI pathway: small data methodology and interpretability
Operating under extreme data scarcity, the imaging pathway fixes a ResNet‑18 backbone for slice‑level feature extraction, aggregates to the subject level via mean pooling, and evaluates with a leave‑two‑out scheme to stabilise estimates. Feature‑space analyses suggest that directional structure (cosine orientation), rather than Euclidean magnitude, carries the discriminative signal; accordingly, kernel PCA improves cluster cohesion relative to linear PCA (silhouette 0.653 vs 0.437). A pre‑specified directionality check and temperature scaling mitigated small‑sample hazards such as logit sign inversion and miscalibration. Grad‑CAM overlays localise attention to sacroiliac joint regions, providing an anatomically plausible link between the embedding geometry and task‑relevant saliency (Selvaraju et al., 2017). Given the micro‑cohort scale, the AUROC ≈ 0.83 should be interpreted as proof‑of‑concept rather than benchmark‑level readiness; recent interpretable radiology pipelines trained on substantially larger datasets have reported accuracies around 87% for sacroiliitis tasks, underscoring the need for multi‑centre MRI expansion, protocol harmonisation, and blinded adjudication to assess generalisability and reader variability (Liu et al., 2024; Kiltz et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2021).

Multimodal integration: There is a growing consensus in the literature that combining clinical and imaging data could yield the best diagnostic performance for AS, but actually realizing this multimodal advantage has proven difficult. A striking example is provided by Warner et al. (2024), who conducted a three-center study with 452 patients having paired MRI and EHR data. They implemented an early-fusion deep learning model (concatenating image and tabular features in one network) and found only a 0.02 AUROC increase compared to using the clinical data alone. This negligible gain came at the cost of tripling the computational burden and significantly worsening calibration on external data. Warner's experience underscores two points echoed by other studies: (1) truly paired multimodal datasets are rare and often small (few hundred cases at best), and (2) naive early fusion may introduce more problems (overfitting, calibration drift) than benefits. Our study was designed with these lessons in mind. We intentionally adopted a late-fusion architecture: the clinical and MRI pipelines were developed in isolation and can be combined at the decision level rather than raw-data level. This modular approach means that each model can be optimized and validated separately, and a simple meta-ensemble (e.g., averaging predictions or training a light secondary classifier on top of their outputs) can integrate them when we eventually have paired cases. Although we could not fully test the multimodal boost due to the current lack of patients with both MRI and EHR in our data, our architecture avoids the early-fusion pitfalls documented by Warner et al. and others. We anticipate that once a sufficiently large paired dataset is acquired, our fusion layer (which is presently dormant) can be activated to learn the optimal weighting of clinical vs. imaging model outputs. In the interim, our dual-pathway system already provides flexibility: for a patient with only clinical data available, ClinicalNet alone can be used; if an MRI is later obtained, the ImagingNet output can be combined without retraining either component. This is similar in spirit to how ensemble classifiers work and aligns with real-world practice (where not all patients get an MRI initially). In comparing with literature, our approach represents an innovative solution to the "non-paired data" problem that has been identified as a major translational hurdle. Rather than wait for the perfect paired dataset or force an early integration on limited data, we demonstrated progress on each modality independently while ensuring the systems are architecturally ready for future fusion. To our knowledge, this explicit decoupling strategy is relatively novel in AS diagnosis literature, where most works are still siloed (only clinical or only imaging) or attempt early fusion without fully solving data mismatches. We believe this modular design will become increasingly important, especially for regulatory approval, because it allows one to validate and certify each component of a multimodal AI separately (as suggested by some modular AI regulatory frameworks). In summary, when contextualized in the literature, our work achieves state-of-the-art performance on the clinical side, introduces a unique and successful small-data imaging paradigm, and proposes a forward-looking multimodal architecture that pre-empts known challenges. These distinctions highlight the novelty and significance of our contributions relative to prior studies. 4.5 Limitations and Future Directions4.5 局限性和未来方向
Notwithstanding the promising results, this study has several important limitations that must be acknowledged. Data representativeness is a primary concern. The clinical data used to train ClinicalNet were derived from a retrospective specialist clinic cohort, which may not perfectly reflect the broader population of patients with back pain or early AS in primary care. For instance, in our balanced dataset the control group (patients with other rheumatic conditions) had an HLA-B27 positivity rate of 25%, far above the ~8% prevalence of HLA-B27 in the general healthy population. This enrichment occurred because our "non-AS" controls often had other spondyloarthropathies or inflammatory conditions that led them to be tested for HLA-B27. While this makes our model more robust in distinguishing AS from conditions that can mimic it (since many controls shared some features like positive B27 or high inflammation), it could also mean the model is tuned to a high-prevalence setting. In a true primary care scenario, where most back pain patients have neither AS nor any immunologic markers, the model's positive predictive value might be lower and its false-positive rate could be higher than reported here. In short, our model needs evaluation on more representative cohorts – for example, a primary care dataset or general population sample – to ensure its performance and calibration hold when the baseline prevalence of AS and distribution of features (e.g., HLA-B27, CRP levels) are different. We partially addressed this by calibrating probabilities and using decision analysis, which can help adjust usage of the model in different prevalence settings, but external validation is indispensable. A related limitation is that we balanced the classes 1:1 via downsampling, which was methodologically appropriate for model training and internal evaluation but departs from the true AS prevalence (~5% or less among chronic back pain patients). As a result, the reported sensitivity and specificity would need recalibration if the model were applied in an unbalanced real-world population. We recommend that future work validate ClinicalNet on an unbalanced sample and potentially apply threshold adjustments or other techniques (like prevalence correction in Bayes' theorem) to tailor it for deployment.尽管结果令人鼓舞，但这项研究仍有几个重要的局限性需要承认。 数据代表性是主要关注点。用于训练 ClinicalNet 的临床数据来自回顾性专科诊所队列，这可能无法完美反映初级保健中患有背痛或早期 AS 的更广泛患者群体。例如，在我们平衡的数据集中，对照组（患有其他风湿病的患者）的 HLA-B27 阳性率为 25%，远高于一般健康人群中 HLA-B27 的约 8% 患病率。出现这种丰富情况是因为我们的"非 AS"对照通常患有其他脊柱关节病或炎症，因此需要接受 HLA-B27 检测。虽然这使得我们的模型在区分 AS 和可能模仿它的疾病方面更加稳健（因为许多对照具有一些共同的特征，例如 B27 阳性或炎症严重），但这也可能意味着该模型针对高患病率环境进行了调整 。在真实的初级保健场景中，大多数背痛患者既没有强直性脊柱炎 (AS)，也没有任何免疫学标志物，因此该模型的阳性预测值可能较低，假阳性率可能高于本文报道的数值。简而言之，我们的模型需要在更具代表性的队列（例如，初级保健数据集或一般人群样本）上进行评估，以确保其性能和校准在强直性脊柱炎 (AS) 的基线患病率和特征分布（例如，HLA-B27、CRP 水平）不同的情况下仍然有效。我们通过校准概率和使用决策分析部分解决了这个问题，这有助于调整模型在不同患病率环境下的使用，但外部验证必不可少。 一个相关的限制是，我们通过下采样以 1:1 的比例平衡了各个类别 ，这在方法论上适合模型训练和内部评估，但却偏离了真实的 AS 患病率（在慢性背痛患者中约为 5% 或更低）。因此，如果将模型应用于不平衡的真实人群，则需要重新校准报告的灵敏度和特异性。我们建议未来的研究在不平衡的样本上验证 ClinicalNet ，并可能应用阈值调整或其他技术（例如贝叶斯定理中的患病率校正）来定制其部署方案。
The MRI dataset we used is extremely small (14 subjects) and was sourced from a public repository (Radiopaedia) that may introduce selection bias. Radiopaedia cases are often illustrative examples of pathology – in our case, the AS patients likely had clear-cut active sacroiliitis, and the healthy controls were distinctly normal – which might not reflect the subtlety spectrum seen in everyday practice. Therefore, the imaging model might be overfitted to prototypical disease presentations and might struggle with borderline or equivocal MRIs that radiologists often see. Additionally, our MRI data were gathered from multiple contributors using possibly different MRI scanners and protocols (the set included both 1.5 Tesla and 3 Tesla MRI scans, with varying image quality and sequences). We did not have enough data to systematically analyze the impact of scanner differences, but such heterogeneity can affect model robustness. In principle, having multi-scanner data could improve generalizability (since the model sees varied inputs), but with so few samples we could not capitalize on this and might have inadvertently learned scanner-specific quirks. The leave-two-out evaluation somewhat mitigates this by ensuring diversity in each fold, but it's no substitute for a large, balanced multi-center dataset. Thus, the imaging results should be interpreted as a proof-of-concept on a convenience sample, not a validated clinical tool. The model's estimated performance (AUROC ~0.83) has a very wide confidence interval and could regress toward 0.5 with different or tougher cases. We stress that no firm clinical conclusions can be drawn until the imaging model is trained and tested on a larger cohort. The next steps should include expanding the MRI sample size substantially – for example, assembling a multi-center dataset of AS MRI scans, ideally with a mix of acute and early cases and controls – to verify whether the performance holds and to enable training of more complex models if needed. Data augmentation strategies (cropping, rotation, synthetic generation of MRI via generative models) could also be employed to expand the effective sample size, though caution is warranted to not introduce false features. Furthermore, advanced techniques like self-supervised learning could be leveraged on unlabelled spine MRI images to pre-train the model on in-domain data before fine-tuning on the small labeled set, which might boost sensitivity to subtle lesions. Another limitation is that we have not yet realized the multimodal fusion of the two pathways on real paired data. Our framework is built to allow fusion, but due to the lack of patients with both EHR and MRI available, we evaluated each modality separately. Consequently, we do not have empirical evidence of actual performance gain from combining modalities in this study. It is possible that when we do obtain a paired dataset, the improvement from fusion may be modest (as seen in Warner et al., 2024). However, given the complementary nature of the data (clinical features capture systemic and historical context, while MRI provides localized objective evidence), we hypothesize that a properly calibrated late-fusion could improve overall diagnostic confidence, even if AUROC gains are small. For example, one can imagine using the clinical model to triage who should get an MRI, and then using the MRI model's result to either confirm the diagnosis or prompt reconsideration. We plan as future work to collect or curate a dataset of paired clinical + MRI cases (perhaps through a prospective study or multi-center collaboration). Even a few hundred paired cases would allow us to train the simple fusion layer (likely a logistic regression or shallow neural net that takes as input the two model outputs and perhaps key features) and evaluate the net benefit of combining modalities. Another future direction is exploring temporal integration: our current clinical model looks at static features or aggregates over time (e.g., max CRP), but incorporating time-series patterns (e.g., changes in inflammatory markers or symptoms over time) via recurrent or transformer models could further improve early detection. We actually tested a time-aware transformer in our experiments (not reported in detail here) and found that, with our data, it did not surpass the simpler MLP – likely due to many patients having only one encounter or the data being too sparse for complex sequence modeling. As longitudinal datasets grow (e.g., repeated primary care visits, serial imaging), more sophisticated temporal models might become worthwhile. This ties into the concept of continuous learning: a deployed AI system could update itself as new data come in, to refine its predictions especially in the face of evolving practice patterns or imaging protocols. From an ethical and regulatory standpoint, we acknowledge several points. All patient data used were de-identified and, in the case of MRI, publicly available with appropriate licenses. We adhered to best practices for transparent reporting (following TRIPOD-AI guidelines for reporting prediction models and performing calibration and decision analyses that regulators and clinicians expect). MRI 而言，在获得适当许可的情况下可公开获取。回顾性临床数据分析获得了机构伦理批准（或适用的豁免），并符合数据保护法规。我们遵循透明报告的最佳实践（遵循 TRIPOD-AI 指南，报告预测模型并执行监管机构和临床医生期望的校准和决策分析）。然而，在临床部署之前，还需要采取进一步措施：前瞻性验证（根据 SPIRIT-AI 的 AI 临床试验指南）和早期部署研究（DECIDE-AI 框架）将有助于评估该模型在处理新鲜实时数据时的表现及其对临床工作流程的影响。我们还认识到，我们的成像组件使用了 Radiopaedia 病例，这些病例带有限制临床使用的知识共享许可证；任何商业或临床应用都需要使用不受此类限制的专有或开放数据进行重新训练。在我们的开发路线图中，我们计划用我们团队正在收集的 AS MRI 内部数据集替换公共 MRI 数据，以确保未来产品不会出现许可问题。 最后，关于算法偏差，虽然我们的数据集无法深入研究亚组表现，但我们意识到人工智能模型可能会继承医疗保健偏差（例如，历史上女性强直性脊柱炎的诊断不足）。我们的模型确实将男性性别识别为与流行病学相符的风险因素，但我们在内部验证中并未观察或测试性别之间的表现差异。随着数据规模的扩大，进行公平性分析（针对性别、种族等）至关重要，并且正如最近的研究表明的那样，可以通过重新加权或对抗性去偏差等技术来潜在地减轻任何差异。确保模型公平地惠及所有患者群体是未来的一个重要目标，尤其是考虑到过时的假设常常会忽略女性强直性脊柱炎。总结本节，概述的局限性会削弱我们的研究结果，但也指明了清晰的未来方向。我们的双通路模型令人鼓舞的结果应该在更多样化、更大规模的队列中得到证实和扩展。后续关键步骤包括在普通人群环境中对 ClinicalNet 进行外部验证，在更大规模的多中心数据上扩展和重新训练 MRI 模型（可能使用联邦学习来克服数据共享问题），并最终在临床工作流程中对组合多模态工具进行前瞻性测试。通过公开承认这些局限性，我们秉持着负责任的 AI 研究不仅应关注其性能，还应批判性地评估其适用性和准备就绪程度的原则。我们发现的每一个局限性都与相应的缓解策略或未来工作构想相结合，体现了我们致力于将这一研究轨迹推进到可临床部署的解决方案的决心。 我们构建的模块化架构将助力后续诸多步骤的推进：例如，临床模型可以随着新患者数据的输入而独立验证或更新，影像模型也可以在不影响其他模型的情况下进行替换或改进。这种灵活性将促成一个迭代式的改进过程。最终，虽然我们目前的研究奠定了基础，但正是这种持续、严谨的改进——与临床医生合作、扩展数据并遵循安全/伦理——将决定人工智能在缓解强直性脊柱炎诊断延迟方面的真正效果。
